# Aggro Combo and Control

This first layer helps a player describe the game they want to create before choosing an archetypical shell. It is not a power ranking or a requirement to choose only one label.

## Aggro

Aggro makes the opponent's remaining time matter. It develops pressure and uses other resources to keep that pressure relevant. An attacker that forces a defensive play can be useful before it deals lethal damage.

In multiplayer Commander, ask how the attack plan continues beyond the first opponent. Early creatures may need replenishment, evasion, scaling, or a later conversion into a shorter clock. Being aggressive does not mean emptying the hand regardless of the board.

Look for supplied bodies, token production, attack rewards, ways through blockers, and ways to keep producing threats. The central question is: **What makes the next attack matter?**

Historical orientation: Reid Duke's [Aggro Decks](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/aggro-decks-2014-09-29) explains early pressure and tempo in 60-card Constructed. Its opening-hand emphasis is a reference point, not a rule that multiplayer aggro should omit sustained card flow.

## Combo

Combo concentrates on interactions whose combined result is stronger than the separate actions. Some produce a large but finite advantage; others can repeat a legal sequence or reach a game-ending result. Neither combo nor engine automatically means infinite.

Ask what starts the sequence, what each step consumes, what it produces, and where it stops. Mana needs an outlet. New creatures need a legal opportunity to attack. Repetition needs resources and permissions that actually renew.

The central question is: **What does this combination accomplish that its pieces cannot accomplish separately?** A casual combo plan can be appropriate when its play pattern fits the group. Do not require or reject one solely because of the label.

## Control

Control manages what opposing resources can accomplish while developing a position it can exploit. Answers may counter spells, remove permanents, restrict actions, deter attacks, or repeatedly undo development. The deck still needs to finish.

In multiplayer, one answer rarely covers every opponent. Decide what needs answering, what can be tolerated, and how the deck develops while preserving a relevant response. Passing with mana available is purposeful only when the available actions justify it.

The central question is: **What are we buying time for?** A board that is difficult to attack is not automatically a board that can win.

Historical orientation: Duke's [Control Decks](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/control-decks-2014-10-06) connects answers and card advantage to a late-game plan. Its list and era-dependent card assessments are historical references, not current recommendations.

## Hybrids

Aggro-control develops a threat and protects its opportunity to attack. Combo-control buys time for a decisive interaction. A midrange deck may defend against a faster start and pressure a slower opponent. An aggressive deck may finish with a combo rather than another combat.

Do not teach a universal rock-paper-scissors matchup chart. Roles change with the hand, board, and opponents. An archetype name is useful context, not proof of a favorable matchup.

Tempo describes a practical lead in usable development and timing. Midrange describes a flexible approach to threats and resource exchanges. Both are useful vocabulary, not extra mandatory layers inserted into the teaching sequence.

## Choosing a Direction

Ask which experience sounds closest: establishing threats and making opponents respond, assembling interacting pieces, or managing opposing plans while preparing a winning position. Then show evidence in the supplied pool.

If several approaches are supported, offer choices. Do not assign identity from a commander's popularity page. Continue to the [Archetypical Shell library](../archetypes/archetype-library.md).
