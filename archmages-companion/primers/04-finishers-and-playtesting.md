# Finishers and Playtesting

A productive engine is not automatically a finished deck. Explain how its output reaches an ending, then test whether ordinary draws can follow that path.

## Finishing Routes

- **Combat conversion:** an established board becomes a short clock through size, evasion, extra attacks, or another supplied effect.
- **Resource conversion:** accumulated mana, cards, or expendable permanents fuel a verified ending.
- **Protected pressure:** threats keep advancing while protection and development make recovery harder for opponents.
- **Closing restriction:** opponents lose the practical opportunity to recover before an existing clock ends the game.
- **Alternate victory:** a supplied effect's actual win conditions and timing are met.

Fit the player's intended experience. Do not replace a preferred combat finish with an unrelated compact combo just because it occupies fewer slots. Do not hide a discovered combo when the player asks what the pool can do; explain it and whether it fits their goals.

## Checking a Win

For combat, identify attack-ready creatures, actual size, blockers, evasion, restrictions, and how many opponents the damage can cover. A board that defeats one player may not defeat the table.

For a loop, list the starting state, costs, zone changes, triggers, targets, mana colors, and net output. Identify its outlet and interruption points. Repetition is not a guarantee of unopposed resolution.

Report whether the interaction is a two-, three-, or four-card core, and whether a separate outlet increases the count for the full win. Include required commanders and enabling permanents. The [combo-structure primer](05-combo-structure.md) provides the accounting format.

For protection, count the mana remaining after the payoff and confirm the protection is actually available. A protection spell somewhere in the library is not protection in hand. Conversely, a hypothetical opposing answer does not by itself refute the plan.

## Testing Lenses

**Opening:** Can an ordinary keep under the agreed mulligan rules make useful early plays in the right colors? Are there functional alternatives to the ideal sequence?

**Development:** If the opening has not ended the game, can the deck keep drawing, deploying, and interacting? Can two smaller plays replace waiting for one expensive payoff?

**Recovery:** Remove an important piece. What can the remaining cards accomplish? What changes after a relevant sweep, graveyard exile, or commander recast? Test plausible disruptions without assuming every opponent has every answer.

Goldfishing checks sequencing and resource feasibility. Opposed games test whether those windows actually exist. One strong hand or one loss does not settle reliability.

Across games, vary ordinary keeps and which engine component appears first. Record whether the same functions remain available through different cards, whether a partial engine is useful, and which resource restrictions actually caused stalls. Keep those observations separate from tournament results cited as historical teaching examples. Adapt the engine to repeated problems while preserving the player's chosen shell and play range.

## Useful Questions

Choose one or two per adjustment:

- Did the draw package function before the hand was exhausted?
- Was there a useful play without the commander?
- Did the finisher advance the game or wait for another piece?
- Did role overlap help, or did the swap remove the only source of a necessary function?
- Did the deck create the intended experience for the group?

Record observations as observations and proposals as proposals. Once a player confirms a change, update the current list in place.
