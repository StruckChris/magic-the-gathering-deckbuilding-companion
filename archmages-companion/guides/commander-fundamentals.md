# Commander Fundamentals

Read this reference for a new Commander build or substantial rebuild, whatever the player's experience level. Use the [Start Here conversation script](../START_HERE.md#deckbuilding-conversation-script) to establish the intended games and preferences before drafting the list. These fundamentals support that process and the main teaching hierarchy; explain only the terms the player needs rather than reciting a checklist.

## Format Checks

An ordinary Commander deck has 100 cards including its commander, uses singleton construction except for basic lands and explicit exceptions, and follows its commander's color identity. Multiplayer games normally begin at 40 life. Legal multi-commander configurations and special card text require checking their specific rules, not inventing a partner pairing. Use the official [Commander overview](https://magic.wizards.com/en/formats/commander), [rules](https://magic.wizards.com/en/rules), and [banned list](https://magic.wizards.com/en/banned-restricted-list).

Color identity is not simply a card's casting cost. Check the entire relevant card and applicable exceptions. A disclosed custom pairing or other house rule can be accepted as the group's agreement without calling it standard legality.

## Useful Vocabulary

| Term | Working explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Mana curve | How the deck's spells and planned actions are distributed across costs |
| Fixing | Making the required colors available, not necessarily increasing total mana |
| Ramp | Increasing available mana beyond ordinary development |
| Card selection | Improving which cards are available, without necessarily increasing their number |
| Card advantage | Gaining more usable card resources than the exchange consumes; explain the actual accounting |
| Tempo | A practical lead in usable development or timing |
| Clock | How quickly the current pressure could end the game under stated assumptions |
| Anthem | An effect that strengthens a group of creatures |
| Evasion | A property or effect that helps an attacker get past blockers |
| Removal | An answer that takes a relevant object out of use; different forms have different limits |
| Recursion | Retrieving or reusing resources from a graveyard |
| Tutor | Searching for a card, often subject to restrictions |
| Mana sink | A useful way to spend additional mana as it becomes available |

Explain only the terms needed for the conversation. Do not treat jargon as evidence of expertise.

## Functional Construction

The deck needs to access and cast its actions, maintain enough resources, interact where its plan requires it, and eventually finish. There is no single compulsory allocation that proves every pool can do these things.

Examine actual costs, color requirements, tapped lands, activation plans, and intended sequencing before making mana suggestions. Early land drops cannot be justified solely by a draw engine that needs several lands before it starts. Mana rocks and land access are not automatically interchangeable.

Consider ordinary draws and recovery after interruption. Cheap cards are not automatically efficient if they do nothing useful here; expensive cards are not automatically unsuitable if their role and deployment are supported.

## Teaching Notes

### Decision Complexity Is Not Missing Support

Distinguish whether a deck has the resources to carry out its plan from how much judgment it takes to use those resources. An opening with an obvious sequence can be easier to navigate than one involving searches, selection, and competing development plays. That does not by itself establish which deck is better supported. Do not mistake a decision-intensive plan for an inadequate one, or assume a simpler plan is inferior.

Land count alone cannot explain the plan. Examine the actual routes to usable mana: colors, timing, search targets, costs to access them, and what those actions leave available for the next play. Lands, land-search effects, fetch lands, mana rocks, and card selection can serve different purposes. An additional role in the engine may matter beyond the immediate mana contribution. Equally, do not count a route as available before the hand can pay for it.

### Game Pace and Mana Timing

The more casual and slower the expected games, the more turns a player generally has to draw and develop lands that supply the specific colored mana pips their spells and abilities require, as well as accumulate enough total mana for their plan. Faster or more competitive games place greater emphasis on hitting particular early land drops and meeting specific midgame mana requirements, including those colored pips, on time. The important question shifts from how much mana the deck can eventually develop to what it needs available at the stages when its plays matter.

Ask when the proposed deck needs to establish its engine, answer opposing development, or deploy a threat while leaving interaction available. Work backward from those plays to the required land drops, colors, and total usable mana. A midgame requirement might include a commander plus an activation or protection, not just the commander's casting cost. Use the player's expected game pace to choose these checkpoints rather than applying one timetable to every deck.

Build the route with the supplied combination of lands, fetch lands, search options, and mana rocks. A fetch land can secure an appropriate color source; a search to hand can supply a later land drop; a search onto the battlefield or a mana rock can advance available mana according to its cost and timing. Check eligible targets, tapped entry, setup costs, and when each source becomes usable. Mana creatures and cost reductions can contribute too. Explain how the package meets the relevant checkpoints, rather than treating land count alone as the answer to a faster game.

### Mana Support Participates in the Deck

Evaluate mana support through three related jobs:

- **Opening development:** accessing the colors and resources needed to begin useful plays.
- **Engine participation:** contributing to the deck's interactions beyond the immediate mana benefit.
- **Recovery:** supplying the resources to rebuild or recast a commander while continuing the plan.

Different packages can serve those jobs differently. A mana creature such as Llanowar Elves is also an Elf: in a supported Elf strategy, its creature type can matter alongside its mana ability. An artifact-focused deck may reuse or otherwise benefit from its mana rocks as artifacts. These are contextual examples, not cards to add outside the supplied pool.

Land search is a broader family than basic-land search. Distinguish basic-only searches, searches with specific land-type or nonbasic restrictions, and searches that can find any land. Check the actual eligible targets in the supplied deck. One plan may prioritize snow basics; another may seek nonbasic color fixing or a utility land that supports its engine. Snow basics illustrate a deck-specific priority, not a default for all searches. Also distinguish finding a land for a later land drop from putting one directly onto the battlefield: what can be found and how it becomes available are separate parts of the evaluation.

Cost reduction belongs in this conversation too. A low-curve deck may still want to deploy several spells and reserve mana for actions on opposing turns. Savings on eligible costs can support that plan without themselves producing mana. Evaluate the intended turn cycle, not just the cost of the most expensive spell.

Connect commander-recasting needs to the question, "What kinds of games do you usually experience?" Establish whether the commander is an essential engine piece, a supporting piece, a finisher, or a combination of roles. Ask what the rest of the deck can accomplish without it and whether the player expects it to attract repeated removal. Commander dependence can change as the deck gains alternative routes; assess the current plan rather than assuming the original dependence remains.

If losing an essential commander interrupts the main engine, additional mana sources beyond lands may deliberately support prompt recasts, with enough left to resume useful actions. A deck whose commander supports or finishes an independently functioning plan may have less urgency to recast it. This is a design consideration, not a universal rule about rock counts. Use the player's description of their usual games, not hypothetical repeated removal, to decide how much emphasis recovery deserves. Consider which sources would remain usable through the disruption they describe. A high rock count alone does not prove the commander's role or explain the builder's original intent.

**Coaching question:** "Which forms of mana support help this deck develop, participate in its engine, and recover?" Use the answer when discussing land counts and swaps. Count overlapping jobs as benefits of the same card, not as independent mana sources.

### Recommend a Starting Land Count

A context-sensitive approach should still produce a concrete recommendation. For a new build, propose a specific provisional land count once the supplied pool, shell, engine, intended play range, and mana preferences are clear. Ask about material unknowns before settling the allocation. Recommend the land count and support package together; do not use a count chosen in advance as the reason to exclude a search, discount, or other alternative without evaluating its role. Explain why that number is a reasonable starting point; do not stop at "it depends" or treat one number as compulsory for every Commander deck. For an existing deck, start by understanding its current count and mana plan rather than resetting it to a generic baseline.

Use this guided process:

1. Review the nonland mana-value distribution and intended opening plays against the expected game pace. Identify important land drops and midgame mana requirements. Account separately for the commander, colored costs, activation costs, and turns that need both development and interaction. A low average mana value alone does not describe the mana needed for several actions in one turn.
2. Identify the supplied land access, early draw or selection, and alternative mana. Explain when each becomes usable, any setup it requires, and its contribution to opening development, engine participation, or recovery. Count actual lands separately from rocks and optional land faces; do not automatically subtract one land per mana rock or cantrip.
3. Before recommending the count, confirm basic-land availability and discuss the player's preferred count, familiarity with the plan, and interest in conditional keeps and sequencing wherever those answers are missing. Include the commander's role and recasting needs in the context of their usual games. Then propose the starting count with its supporting package and assumptions. Intended power level informs the conversation but does not determine the count by itself.
4. Only propose a reduction after reviewing the curve and mana plan and discussing it with the player. Name the specific slots, the benefit gained, and the opening or sequencing conditions the lower count asks the player to recognize. Explain why the available support can function before the mana it is meant to supply is needed. Lowering the count is an option, not the goal of refinement; retaining or increasing it can also fit the plan.

Keep the agreed count provisional when the deck is still being assembled. Help the player refine it as their understanding of the deck grows, without requiring a game log. Proposed changes remain proposals until accepted. Use the [mana-plan conversation prompts](../templates/intake-and-review.md#mana-plan-conversation) alongside questions about the desired games. The wording is flexible and known answers need not be repeated; material unanswered preferences still belong before the full-list recommendation.

### Competitive Context for Lower Land Counts

Competitive deck development provides examples of leaner mana bases as particular shells adopt cheaper actions and more efficient support. Use the specific lineage to explain how the curve and mana plan evolved together.

For a historical comparison, Paul Sligh's 1996 list is reproduced with **23 lands in 61 cards**, including three- and four-mana creatures. [Alex Shvartsman's 2004 retrospective](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/famous-red-decks-magic-history-2004-07-22-0) explains the mana-curve approach and its later shift toward focused beatdown. Tom Ross's pre-M15 Boss Sligh list used **17 lands in 60 cards**, with many cheap actions; his proposed M15 revision increased that to **18**. [Ross's firsthand 2014 article](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/boss-sligh-in-m15/) illustrates both a leaner later branch and continued adjustment. Some competitive decks may still use high land counts to support their plans, but there is a downward trend across competitive decks. That does not mean casual commander decks should follow lower land counts that may be difficult for a new player to manage. Taking into consideration what level of commander game and the goals of the player are part of managing the deckbuilding process, which can be refined later. 

Connect that history to learning the deck. Recognizing which hands contain the resources for its actual plan, and which should be mulliganed, matters alongside the construction itself. [Reid Duke's 2015 Constructed mulligan lesson](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/mulligans-part-iii-constructed-2015-06-29) ties keep decisions to deck knowledge, early requirements, and the cost of going down cards. Teach that reasoning, not its old mulligan procedure or tournament-speed assumptions. Verify the applicable current rules when needed.

For casual Commander, match the land count to the player's own curve, support, and preferred keep and sequencing decisions. Players do not need to adopt competitive land counts or the same demands on managing their openings. [Duke's 2014 mana-base guide](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/building-mana-base-2014-11-24) also shows why access to the right colors at the right time must be considered separately from the total number of lands. Historical cards are reference examples, not suggested purchases.

### Teach Keep Decisions and Sequencing

Mulligan decisions are part of learning a deck, not automatically a construction failure that must be eliminated. A player may enjoy evaluating conditional hands or prefer more straightforward decisions. Ask about that preference when it materially affects a recommendation; do not infer it from experience level. Neither willingness to mulligan nor an attractive best-case hand proves that a mana plan is supported.

When a player asks for help with an opening, reason from the supplied hand and deck:

- What useful opening does this hand already support, without assuming an ideal draw?
- Which color, land drop, or other resource must become available next, and what actual route supplies it?
- When should a search or mana rock be used, and what other play does that delay?
- What makes keeping this hand reasonable, and which missing condition would favor a mulligan?

Explain the decision rather than just announcing keep or mulligan. Developing a useful position is enough; an opening does not need to threaten an early win. Do not prescribe the author's land counts, a universal target, or repeated mulligans toward one ideal sequence.

### Competing Plays Are Part of Learning

Having to choose between developing the board, supplying an engine, and holding interaction is not inherently a defect to remove. Explain what each line gains now, what it leaves available later, and which visible conditions favor it. Offer a short decision tree when useful, not one supposedly correct sequence for every game.

When suggesting a swap, distinguish a genuine resource gap from a preference for fewer decisions. Explain how the change affects available lines and costs, not only the card count. Teach the player to recognize their options without requiring playtest logs or game records.

## Multiplayer Expectations

Ask what the group enjoys when it affects a recommendation: pace, repeated loops, extra turns, resource denial, or other relevant play patterns. Do not infer preferences from the word casual alone. Matchmaking labels can help start a discussion, but do not establish a precise win rate or replace a description of what the deck does.

Game Changers are an official designation; banned cards are a legality question. "Staple" and "watch card" are informal descriptions. The [four built-in references](../references/opponent-card-reference-guide.md) help explain cards across the table without treating a label as an automatic threat assessment or a reason to buy the same card.

A site's Commander sideboard or considering section may be a personal holding area. Clarify its meaning before treating those cards as a legal event sideboard, available spares, or planned additions.
